What is dementia?

Dementia isn't one disease. Here's what the word really means, how it differs from Alzheimer's, and the main types, explained simply.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 18, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This article is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. Read our full disclaimer.

The short answer

Dementia is a general word for a loss of memory, thinking, and reasoning that is serious enough to get in the way of daily life. It can also change mood, personality, and behavior.

Dementia is not a disease by itself. It's an umbrella term, a bit like "heart disease." Many different conditions can cause it. Alzheimer's disease is the most common one.

Dementia vs. Alzheimer's: what's the difference?

Think of it this way:

  • Dementia describes the symptoms: memory loss, confusion, trouble with everyday tasks.
  • Alzheimer's is one specific disease that causes those symptoms. It causes 60 to 80 percent of dementia cases.

So everyone with Alzheimer's has dementia, but not everyone with dementia has Alzheimer's. Knowing the type matters, because the symptoms, the treatments, and even which medicines are safe can be different.

Is it just part of getting old?

No. Dementia becomes more common with age, but it is not a normal part of aging. Many people reach their 90s and beyond without it.

  • Normal aging: sometimes forgetting a name or appointment and remembering it later. Occasionally needing help with a new gadget.
  • Possible dementia: forgetting recent events often, repeating questions, getting lost in a familiar place, or not being able to manage bills or medicines that used to be easy.

The main types of dementia

  • Alzheimer's disease. The most common type. Protein build-up (amyloid plaques and tau tangles) slowly damages brain cells. It usually starts with trouble remembering recent things. Read our full guide to Alzheimer's.
  • Vascular dementia. Caused by reduced blood flow to the brain, often after strokes or from damaged blood vessels. Problems with planning, focus, and slowed thinking may show up more than memory loss. Symptoms can get worse in sudden steps.
  • Lewy body dementia. Caused by protein clumps called Lewy bodies. Common signs are seeing things that aren't there, good days and bad days, acting out dreams in sleep, and stiffness or shuffling like Parkinson's. People with this type can react badly to some medicines, so it's important to have the right diagnosis.
  • Frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Damages the front and side parts of the brain. It often starts younger, between 45 and 64. Early signs are usually changes in personality, behavior, or language rather than memory.
  • Mixed dementia. More than one type at once, most often Alzheimer's and vascular dementia together. This is common in people over 80.

Some causes can be treated

Not every memory problem is dementia. Some conditions cause similar symptoms and can get better with treatment:

  • Side effects of medicines, or mixing medicines
  • Thyroid problems
  • Low vitamin B12
  • Depression
  • Infections. In older adults, a urinary tract infection can cause sudden confusion.
  • Sleep problems such as sleep apnea
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus (extra fluid in the brain)

Sudden confusion that starts over hours or days is called delirium. It needs a doctor the same day. It's often caused by an infection, dehydration, or a medicine, and it can usually be treated.

How many people have dementia?

The World Health Organization estimates that more than 55 million people worldwide live with dementia. In the U.S., about 7.2 million people 65 and older live with Alzheimer's alone.

What to do if you're worried

  • Write down what you've noticed. Note what changed, when it started, and examples. This helps the doctor a lot.
  • Make a doctor's appointment and bring a list of all medicines, vitamins, and supplements.
  • Ask about tests that rule out treatable causes, and ask for a referral to a memory specialist if needed.
  • Get support early. The Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline, 1-800-272-3900, helps with all types of dementia, not just Alzheimer's.
