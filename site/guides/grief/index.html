Grief, before and after the loss

Alzheimer's is sometimes called "the long goodbye." Many caregivers grieve for years: for the person's memories, their old relationship, and the future they planned. Grief often continues after the death. All of these feelings are normal.

Grieving while they're still here

You may grieve each loss as it comes: the day they forget your name, the day they move to memory care, the last real conversation. This is sometimes called anticipatory grief. You might feel sad, angry, guilty, numb, or even relieved, sometimes all in one day.

What helps:

  • Name the feeling. "I'm grieving" is true even though they're still alive.
  • Talk with people who understand, like a caregiver support group.
  • Make room for joy. Moments of connection still happen, and it's okay to enjoy them.

After the death

  • Relief is normal. Feeling relief that their suffering, or your caregiving, has ended doesn't mean you loved them less.
  • Your days may feel empty. Caregiving may have filled your life for years. It can take time to find a new routine.
  • Take care of your health. See your own doctor. Grief can affect sleep, appetite, and health.
  • Hospice grief support is offered to families for about a year after a hospice death, even if you didn't use all of hospice's services.

When to get more help

Talk to a doctor or counselor if, after several months, you can't do daily tasks, feel hopeless, or can't stop thinking about the loss. If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline any time.

Sources
