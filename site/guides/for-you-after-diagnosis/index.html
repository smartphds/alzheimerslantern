If you've just been diagnosed: a guide for you

This guide is for you, the person who has just heard the words "Alzheimer's" or "dementia." It's normal to feel shocked, scared, angry, or even relieved to finally have an answer. Here are steps that help many people feel more in control.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Give yourself time

You don't have to do everything this week. Many people say the first few weeks feel like a fog. Talk to someone you trust about how you feel. That can be family, a friend, a faith leader, or a counselor.

Alzheimer's usually changes slowly. Many people live well for years after an early diagnosis, keep working on things they love, and stay part of family life.

Things to do while it's easier

  • Ask your doctor your questions. Bring a written list and a family member to take notes. Our questions to ask the doctor list can help.
  • Make your wishes known. Choose who will speak for you about money and medical care if you can't. See our guide on power of attorney and advance directives.
  • Simplify money. Set up automatic bill payments and ask a trusted person to look over statements with you. People with dementia are often targets for scams.
  • Talk about driving. Ask your doctor whether it's still safe, and plan for other ways to get around. See when to stop driving.
  • Think about clinical trials. Some research studies are looking for people in the early stage. Your doctor or ClinicalTrials.gov can tell you more.

Everyday tips that help

  • Keep a daily routine. Do harder tasks at the time of day you feel sharpest.
  • Use a big calendar, a notebook, phone reminders, and a pill organizer.
  • Put keys, wallet, and glasses in the same spot every time.
  • Keep moving. Walk, garden, dance, or do what you enjoy. Stay social.
  • Tell people what helps you, such as "Please speak slowly" or "Give me a moment to find the word."

If you live alone

Many people with early dementia live alone for a time. Ask someone to check in daily, keep a list of emergency numbers by the phone, and consider a medical ID bracelet. The National Institute on Aging has tips for living alone with early-stage dementia.

You are not alone

Early-stage support groups bring together people who are living with a diagnosis, not just caregivers. The Alzheimer's Association's 24/7 Helpline, 1-800-272-3900, can help you find one. You can also look at our support groups guide.
