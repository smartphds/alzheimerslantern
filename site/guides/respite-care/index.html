Finding respite care

Respite care gives caregivers a break, for a few hours or a few days, while someone else safely cares for your loved one. Here's what's available and how to find it.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

What respite care is

"Respite" means a rest. Respite care is any short-term care that gives the regular caregiver time off. Taking breaks helps prevent caregiver burnout, and many caregivers say it helps them keep going longer.

Types of respite

  • In-home respite. A trained aide, volunteer, or family member comes to the home so you can run errands, rest, or see friends.
  • Adult day programs. Your loved one spends the day at a center with activities, meals, and supervision.
  • Short stays. Some assisted living communities and nursing homes offer stays of a few days or weeks, for example while you travel or recover from surgery.
  • Informal help. Friends, neighbors, or faith communities who take turns sitting with your loved one.

How to find it

  • Eldercare Locator: 1-800-677-1116, or eldercare.acl.gov, to find your local Area Agency on Aging
  • ARCH National Respite Network, which has a respite locator by state
  • Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline: 1-800-272-3900
  • Your loved one's doctor or social worker

How to pay for it

  • Medicaid home and community-based waiver programs in many states
  • The National Family Caregiver Support Program, through your Area Agency on Aging, which may offer some free or low-cost respite
  • Veterans' benefits. The VA offers respite care for eligible veterans.
  • Medicare hospice includes short respite stays for people in hospice
  • Long-term care insurance, if the person has a policy
  • Medicare's GUIDE dementia program, in participating areas, can include some respite

Making it go smoothly

  • Start with short breaks so your loved one can get used to new people and places.
  • Write down routines, likes, dislikes, and calming tricks for the respite provider.
  • Expect some upset at first. It often gets easier with time.
  • Use the time for yourself, not only for chores.

Sources

  • National Institute on Aging: What Is Respite Care?
  • ARCH National Respite Network
  • Eldercare Locator
