Bathing without a battle

Bathing is one of the hardest daily tasks for people with Alzheimer's and their caregivers. Here's why it can be upsetting and simple ways to make it calmer and safer.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why bathing can be upsetting

To a person with Alzheimer's, a bath or shower can feel cold, scary, and confusing. They may feel embarrassed being undressed, frightened by running water, or not understand why they need to wash. Refusing a bath is common and usually comes from fear, not stubbornness.

Get ready first

  • Pick their best time of day. If they always bathed in the morning, keep it that way.
  • Warm the room and the towels.
  • Have everything ready: towels, soap, clean clothes, all within reach.
  • Check the water temperature with your elbow or a thermometer. Older skin burns easily.
  • Make it safe: a non-slip mat, grab bars, a shower chair, and a handheld shower head.

During the bath

  • Explain one step at a time: "Let's wash your arms now."
  • Let them do what they can, even if it takes longer.
  • Protect their privacy. Keep a towel over their shoulders or lap.
  • Use a gentle spray, or a washcloth, rather than water in the face.
  • Stay calm and warm, and talk about something pleasant.
  • Never leave the person alone in the tub or shower.

If they refuse

  • Don't force it. Try again in 15 to 30 minutes, or at another time.
  • Change the words: "Let's freshen up" or "The doctor said a warm wash would help your back."
  • A sponge bath in a warm room is fine on many days. Most people don't need a full bath every day. Two or three times a week is often enough.
  • Play their favorite music.
  • Ask whether someone else, like a home health aide, might have better luck.

After the bath

  • Dry well, especially skin folds, to prevent sores.
  • Check the skin for redness, rashes, or sores.
  • Use lotion on dry skin.
  • Help them dress in clothes laid out in order.

Sources

  • National Institute on Aging: Alzheimer's Caregiving: Bathing, Dressing, and Grooming
