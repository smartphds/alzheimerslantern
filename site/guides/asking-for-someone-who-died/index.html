What to say when they ask for someone who has died

When a person with Alzheimer's asks where their late husband or mother is, the truth can feel like hearing the news for the first time. Here are gentle ways to respond.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 4-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why it happens

Alzheimer's affects recent memories first. A person may remember their spouse or parent as still alive, and look for them, sometimes every day. Each time you say "she died," they may grieve as if it just happened.

Listen for the feeling

The question often means something more:

  • "Where's my mother?" may mean "I feel scared" or "I want comfort."
  • "When is John coming home?" may mean "I miss him" or "I feel lonely."

Responding to the feeling usually helps more than the facts.

Gentle ways to respond

  • Ask about the person: "Tell me about your mother. What was she like?"
  • Share a memory: "John loved this song, didn't he?"
  • Comfort: "You miss him. I'm here with you."
  • Redirect kindly: "Let's look at some pictures of your family," or offer a snack or walk.
  • Use a soft, true answer when you can: "He's not here right now."

Should I tell the truth?

There's no single right answer. In the early stage, honesty is usually best, said with care. Later on, many families and experts find that repeating painful news causes more harm than good. Choosing a kind, comforting answer is not lying to hurt. It's meeting the person where they are.

If the person seems to sense the truth or asks directly, it's okay to answer gently: "Your mother died a long time ago. She loved you very much."

Take care of yourself

Hearing these questions can be painful, especially if you're grieving the same person. It's okay to step away for a moment, and to talk with someone who understands.
