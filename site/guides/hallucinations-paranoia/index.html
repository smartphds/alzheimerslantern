Hallucinations, paranoia, and false beliefs

Some people with Alzheimer's see or hear things that aren't there, or believe things that aren't true, like someone is stealing from them. This is the disease changing how the brain makes sense of the world. Here's how to respond kindly.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

What these words mean

  • Hallucinations: seeing, hearing, or feeling things that aren't there, like a stranger in the room.
  • Delusions: firmly believing something untrue, like "My spouse is an imposter."
  • Paranoia: suspicion, often accusing others of stealing or lying.

Accusations of stealing often happen because they put something away and forgot where.

Rule out other causes first

Tell the doctor about any new hallucinations. Infections, dehydration, medicine side effects, and poor eyesight or hearing can all cause them. Vivid hallucinations early on can also be a sign of Lewy body dementia, which needs careful medicine choices.

How to respond

  • Don't argue. You can't talk them out of it, and arguing makes them more upset.
  • Respond to the feeling. "That sounds scary. I'm here and you're safe."
  • Help look for missing things. Learn their usual hiding spots. Keep spare keys and glasses.
  • Check the room. Shadows, mirrors, TV, and patterns on walls can look like people. Better lighting or covering mirrors may help.
  • Distract gently with an activity, a walk, or music.
  • Don't take accusations personally. It's the disease talking.

When it's not a problem

If the hallucination isn't upsetting them, like a friendly cat they think they see, you may not need to do anything.

When to get help

Call the doctor if hallucinations are new, frightening, or leading to unsafe behavior. Call 911 if anyone is in danger.
