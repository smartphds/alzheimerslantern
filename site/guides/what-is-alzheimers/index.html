What is Alzheimer's disease?

A plain-English explanation of what Alzheimer's is, what it does to the brain, how it changes over time, and what can help.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 18, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This article is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. Read our full disclaimer.

The short answer

Alzheimer's disease is a brain disease that slowly damages memory, thinking, and, over time, the ability to do everyday things. It is the most common cause of dementia. It causes an estimated 60 to 80 percent of all dementia cases.

It is not a normal part of getting older. Many people live into their 90s with sharp minds. When memory problems keep getting worse and start to get in the way of daily life, something more than aging is going on.

How common is it?

  • About 7.2 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's. That's about 1 in 9 people in that age group.
  • Almost two out of three Americans with Alzheimer's are women.
  • Nearly 12 million family members and friends give unpaid care to someone with Alzheimer's or another dementia.

If you're caring for someone, you are far from alone.

What happens in the brain

In 1906, a German doctor named Alois Alzheimer studied the brain of a woman who had died after years of memory loss and confusion. He found two kinds of damage that are still the hallmarks of the disease today:

  • Plaques. Clumps of a protein called beta-amyloid build up between brain cells.
  • Tangles. Twisted fibers of another protein, called tau, build up inside brain cells.

Together, these get in the way of how brain cells talk to each other. Over time, the cells lose their connections and die, and the brain shrinks.

The damage usually starts in the hippocampus, the part of the brain that forms new memories. That's why forgetting recent things is often the first sign. Later it spreads to areas that handle language, reasoning, and behavior, and eventually to areas that control basic body functions.

These brain changes can start 10 to 20 years before anyone notices a problem.

Early signs to watch for

Everyone forgets things sometimes. Signs that may point to something more include:

  • Forgetting recent conversations or events, and asking the same question again and again
  • Trouble planning, following a recipe, or keeping track of bills
  • Getting lost in familiar places
  • Trouble finding the right words
  • Misplacing things in odd places, like keys in the freezer
  • Poor judgment, such as falling for scams
  • Pulling away from hobbies or friends
  • Changes in mood or personality, such as becoming confused, suspicious, or anxious

If you notice several of these, talk to a doctor. Some memory problems are caused by treatable conditions, and an early diagnosis gives more time to plan.

The stages

Alzheimer's gets worse over time, but everyone moves through it differently. On average, people live 4 to 8 years after diagnosis, and some live 20 years or more.

  • Early stage (mild). The person can still drive, work, and socialize. They may struggle with names, recent events, or planning. This is the best time to make legal and money plans together.
  • Middle stage (moderate). Usually the longest stage. The person needs more help with daily tasks, may get confused about where they are, may wander, and may have mood and behavior changes.
  • Late stage (severe). The person needs help around the clock with eating, moving, and personal care, and may lose the ability to have a conversation.

Who gets it? Risk factors

There usually isn't one single cause. Things that raise the risk include:

  • Age. This is the biggest risk factor. Most people are diagnosed after 65. Rarely, it begins in a person's 30s, 40s, or 50s. This is called early-onset Alzheimer's.
  • Family history and genes. Having a parent or sibling with Alzheimer's raises your risk. A gene called APOE-e4 raises risk, but it doesn't mean a person will definitely get the disease.
  • Heart and body health. High blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, inactivity, untreated hearing loss, and social isolation are all linked to higher dementia risk.

Good news: a major 2024 report in The Lancet estimated that nearly half of dementia cases worldwide might be prevented or delayed by managing 14 risk factors. These include blood pressure, hearing, physical activity, and staying socially connected.

How it's diagnosed

There's no single simple test. A doctor usually:

  • Asks about symptoms and medical history, and talks with family
  • Gives memory and thinking tests
  • Orders blood tests to rule out other causes, such as thyroid problems or low vitamin B12
  • May order a brain scan (MRI, CT, or PET), a spinal fluid test, or one of the newer blood tests that look for Alzheimer's proteins

Ask for a referral to a neurologist, geriatrician, or memory clinic if you want a more detailed evaluation.

Treatment

There is no cure yet, but there are treatments that can help.

  • Medicines that ease symptoms. Donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, and memantine can help memory and thinking for a while in some people.
  • Newer medicines that slow the disease. Lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla) remove amyloid from the brain. They can modestly slow decline in people at the early stage. They are given by IV infusion, need regular brain scans, and can cause side effects such as brain swelling or small bleeds. They are not right for everyone.
  • Care that makes every day better. A steady routine, a safe home, good sleep, exercise, music, and staying connected with people all make a real difference, at every stage.

Ask the doctor which options fit your situation, and what the benefits and risks are.

What to do next

  • Worried about someone? Write down the changes you've noticed and when they started, and bring the list to a doctor.
  • Just diagnosed? Call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900. It's free, and you can call any time.
