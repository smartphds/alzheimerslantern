When to stop driving

A diagnosis doesn't always mean someone must stop driving right away, but Alzheimer's will eventually make driving unsafe. Here are the warning signs and how to have the conversation.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why driving gets risky

Driving takes quick thinking, judgment, and memory of routes and rules. Alzheimer's slowly affects all of these. People in the early stage may still drive safely for a while, but that changes over time, often before the person notices.

Warning signs

  • Getting lost on familiar routes
  • Missing stop signs or traffic lights
  • Drifting into other lanes, or hitting curbs
  • Confusing the gas and brake pedals
  • New dents or scrapes on the car
  • Taking much longer to make a short trip
  • Getting angry or confused while driving
  • Close calls, tickets, or other drivers honking more often

If you're not sure, ride along a few times and watch.

Getting an outside opinion

  • Ask the doctor to talk about driving. Many people accept it better from a doctor.
  • Ask about a driving evaluation by an occupational therapist or driver rehabilitation specialist.
  • Check your state's rules. Some states require doctors to report certain conditions.

How to have the conversation

  • Start early, ideally soon after the diagnosis, before there's a crisis.
  • Talk about it as safety, not failure: "I want you to stay safe, and others too."
  • Involve the person in planning when to stop and how they'll get around.
  • Expect strong feelings. Driving can mean independence and pride. Grief and anger are normal.
  • Have a plan ready for rides: family, friends, volunteer drivers, senior transportation, ride services, or public transit.

If they keep driving when it's unsafe

  • Ask the doctor to write a "do not drive" prescription.
  • Keep the car keys out of sight.
  • Disable the car or ask a mechanic for help.
  • Sell or move the car, if needed.

It's hard, but it may save a life.

  • National Institute on Aging: Driving Safety and Alzheimer's Disease
