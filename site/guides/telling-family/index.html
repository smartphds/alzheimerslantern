Telling family and friends

Sharing an Alzheimer's diagnosis can feel hard. Being open often brings more support, not less. Here's how to decide who to tell and what to say.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why telling people helps

Keeping a diagnosis secret takes a lot of energy. When people know, they're more likely to be patient, to offer help, and to understand changes they might otherwise take personally. Telling people is also a way to ask for the support you'll need later.

Who to tell first

  • Close family who will be part of care and decisions
  • Close friends you see often
  • People who can help with safety, such as neighbors
  • Work, clubs, or faith community, when you're ready

If the person with the diagnosis is able, let them decide who to tell and how. It's their news.

What to say

Keep it simple and honest. For example:

  • "I've been diagnosed with Alzheimer's. I'm still me. I may need a little more patience and help as time goes on."
  • "Mom has been diagnosed with Alzheimer's. She's doing okay, and she'd love to keep seeing you. Here's how you can help."

Tell them how to help, because most people don't know:

  • "Call or visit. Short visits are great."
  • "Please don't quiz her on names. Just say who you are."
  • "We could use help with rides to appointments on Thursdays."

Telling children and teens

  • Use simple words that fit their age: "Grandpa has an illness that makes it hard for his brain to remember things."
  • Reassure them that it's not their fault and they can't catch it.
  • Let them ask questions and share feelings.
  • Give them simple ways to stay close: looking at photos, singing, going for a walk.

How people may react

Some will step up. Some may pull away because they feel awkward or sad. It's often not about you. Give people time, and lean on those who show up.

Sources

  • National Institute on Aging: Helping Family and Friends Understand Alzheimer's Disease
