MCI or dementia: what's the difference?

Mild cognitive impairment, or MCI, sits between normal aging and dementia. Here's what it means, how it differs from dementia, and what to do next.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

What is MCI?

Mild cognitive impairment means a person has more trouble with memory or thinking than is normal for their age, but they can still handle everyday life on their own. Others may notice the changes, but the person can still drive, manage money, and take care of themselves.

How it differs from dementia

  • Normal aging: occasional forgetfulness, like misplacing keys or blanking on a name, that doesn't get worse over time.
  • MCI: noticeable problems, such as often forgetting appointments or conversations, but daily life still works.
  • Dementia: problems serious enough that the person needs help with everyday tasks, such as bills, medicines, cooking, or getting around. See What is dementia?

Will MCI turn into Alzheimer's?

Not always. Some people with MCI go on to develop Alzheimer's or another dementia. Others stay the same for years, and some even get better, especially when the cause is treatable.

MCI can be caused by:

  • Early Alzheimer's disease
  • Small strokes or blood vessel problems
  • Medicine side effects
  • Depression, anxiety, or stress
  • Poor sleep or sleep apnea
  • Thyroid problems or low vitamin B12

That's why a full check-up matters.

What to do

  • See a doctor for a full evaluation, and ask what might be causing the changes.
  • Ask about follow-up. Many doctors recheck memory every 6 to 12 months to see if anything changes.
  • Ask about treatment. If MCI is caused by Alzheimer's, the newer medicines that slow the disease may be an option. See New Alzheimer's treatments in plain English.
  • Protect your brain health: stay active, treat high blood pressure and diabetes, get hearing checked, sleep well, don't smoke, and stay social.
  • Plan ahead anyway. Setting up a power of attorney and advance directive is wise for everyone.
