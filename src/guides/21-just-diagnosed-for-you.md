## Give yourself time

You don't have to do everything this week. Many people say the first few weeks feel like a fog. Talk to someone you trust about how you feel. That can be family, a friend, a faith leader, or a counselor.

Alzheimer's usually changes slowly. Many people live well for years after an early diagnosis, keep working on things they love, and stay part of family life.

## Things to do while it's easier

- **Ask your doctor your questions.** Bring a written list and a family member to take notes. Our [questions to ask the doctor](#questions-for-doctor) list can help.
- **Make your wishes known.** Choose who will speak for you about money and medical care if you can't. See our guide on [power of attorney and advance directives](#power-of-attorney).
- **Simplify money.** Set up automatic bill payments and ask a trusted person to look over statements with you. People with dementia are often targets for scams.
- **Talk about driving.** Ask your doctor whether it's still safe, and plan for other ways to get around. See [when to stop driving](#driving).
- **Think about clinical trials.** Some research studies are looking for people in the early stage. Your doctor or ClinicalTrials.gov can tell you more.

## Everyday tips that help

- Keep a daily routine. Do harder tasks at the time of day you feel sharpest.
- Use a big calendar, a notebook, phone reminders, and a pill organizer.
- Put keys, wallet, and glasses in the same spot every time.
- Keep moving. Walk, garden, dance, or do what you enjoy. Stay social.
- Tell people what helps you, such as "Please speak slowly" or "Give me a moment to find the word."

## If you live alone

Many people with early dementia live alone for a time. Ask someone to check in daily, keep a list of emergency numbers by the phone, and consider a medical ID bracelet. The National Institute on Aging has [tips for living alone with early-stage dementia](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-and-dementia/tips-living-alone-early-stage-dementia).

## You are not alone

Early-stage support groups bring together people who are living with a diagnosis, not just caregivers. The Alzheimer's Association's 24/7 Helpline, 1-800-272-3900, can help you find one. You can also look at our [support groups guide](#support-groups).
