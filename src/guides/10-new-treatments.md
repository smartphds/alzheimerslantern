## Two kinds of Alzheimer's medicines

**1. Medicines that ease symptoms.** These have been used for many years. They don't change the course of the disease, but they can help memory, thinking, or behavior for a while in some people.

- **Donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine** are used in mild to moderate (and sometimes severe) stages.
- **Memantine** is used in moderate to severe stages, sometimes together with donepezil.
- Common side effects include upset stomach, loss of appetite, dizziness, and sleep changes.

**2. Medicines that slow the disease.** These are newer and work in a different way. They target **amyloid**, the sticky protein that builds up in the brains of people with Alzheimer's.

## The newer medicines

- **Lecanemab (brand name Leqembi)**, approved by the FDA in 2023
- **Donanemab (brand name Kisunla)**, approved by the FDA in 2024

In large studies, both **modestly slowed** the decline of memory and thinking over about 18 months in people with early Alzheimer's, compared with people who got a placebo. They do not stop the disease or bring back lost memory.

## Who they're for

- People with **mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia** caused by Alzheimer's
- Amyloid must be **confirmed** with a PET scan or a spinal fluid test
- They're **not** for people in the moderate or late stage

## How they're given

- Usually by **IV infusion** at a clinic, every 2 weeks (lecanemab) or every 4 weeks (donanemab)
- Regular **MRI brain scans** are needed to check for side effects

## Side effects to know about

The main risk is called **ARIA**: swelling or small bleeds in the brain. Most of the time it causes no symptoms and goes away, but it can be serious, and rarely life-threatening.

- People who carry two copies of a gene called **APOE-e4** have a higher risk. Doctors may suggest a genetic test first.
- People who take **blood thinners** may face extra risk.
- Call the doctor right away about new headache, confusion, dizziness, vision changes, or nausea.

## Questions to ask the doctor

- Is this treatment an option for us? Why or why not?
- What tests do we need first?
- How much benefit might we expect, and how will we know if it's working?
- What are the risks for us, given our genes and other medicines?
- How often are infusions and MRIs, and where?
- What will Medicare or our insurance cover, and what will we pay?

## Other ways to help

Whatever treatment you choose, everyday things still matter a lot: exercise, sleep, social time, treating hearing loss, controlling blood pressure, and staying engaged. Clinical trials are also testing many new treatments. Ask the doctor, or search ClinicalTrials.gov.
