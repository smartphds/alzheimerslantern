## Why they refuse

- **They may not know they're ill.** The disease can damage the part of the brain that lets someone see their own changes. This isn't stubbornness or denial.
- **They want to stay independent**, like anyone would.
- **They feel embarrassed, scared, or rushed.**
- **They don't understand** what you're asking.

## What often helps

- **Don't argue about the diagnosis.** You don't need them to agree that they have Alzheimer's.
- **Frame help as something else.** "The doctor wants to check your blood pressure." "This helper is here to help me with the house."
- **Give simple choices.** "Do you want to shower now or after breakfast?" rather than "Do you want to shower?"
- **Pick your timing.** Try again later, when they're rested and calm.
- **Use a trusted messenger.** Sometimes a doctor, pastor, or old friend gets a yes when family doesn't.
- **Start small.** A helper for one hour a week, growing slowly, is easier than a big change.
- **Focus on what matters most.** Safety and health come first. Let small things go.

## For doctor visits

Book the visit, then tell them about it shortly before you go. Call the office ahead and share your concerns privately. Bring our [questions to ask the doctor](#questions-for-doctor).

## When safety is at risk

If they refuse care and are in danger, such as not eating, leaving the stove on, or getting lost, talk to their doctor, or call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to find local help.
