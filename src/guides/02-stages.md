## Everyone's path is different

Alzheimer's gets worse over time, but at a different pace for each person. On average, people live **4 to 8 years** after a diagnosis, and some live 20 years or more. Stages overlap, and a person may show signs of two stages at once. Use these as a rough map, not a timetable.

Doctors sometimes use a seven-stage scale. Most families find the simpler three stages below easier to follow.

## Before symptoms: the hidden stage

Changes in the brain can begin **10 to 20 years** before anyone notices a problem. Researchers call this "preclinical" Alzheimer's. Some people then have **mild cognitive impairment (MCI)**: small memory or thinking problems that others may notice but that don't stop daily life. Not everyone with MCI goes on to develop Alzheimer's.

## Early stage (mild)

**What it can look like:**

- Trouble remembering names or recent conversations
- Losing or misplacing things more often
- Trouble planning, organizing, or managing money
- Taking longer to finish everyday tasks
- Still driving, working, and socializing, often well

**What helps:**

- Make legal, money, and care plans **now**, while the person can take part and say what they want. See [Power of attorney and advance directives](#power-of-attorney).
- Ask the doctor about treatments. The newer drugs that slow the disease are only for the early stage.
- Keep active, social, and engaged. Exercise, hobbies, and friends all help.
- Use memory aids: a large calendar, pill organizers, phone reminders.
- Join a support group, for the person with the diagnosis and for family.

## Middle stage (moderate)

This is usually the **longest stage** and can last many years. The person needs more and more help.

**What it can look like:**

- Forgetting events or parts of their own history
- Confusion about where they are or what day it is
- Needing help choosing clothes, bathing, or using the toilet
- Changes in sleep, such as sleeping during the day and being restless at night
- Wandering and getting lost
- Mood and behavior changes: suspicion, repeating questions, frustration, or agitation, often worse in the evening ([sundowning](#sundowning))

**What helps:**

- A steady daily routine
- Simple, one-step instructions and calm [communication](#communication)
- Making the home safe and planning for [wandering](#wandering)
- Getting help for yourself: adult day programs, in-home help, and respite care
- Talking about future care options before a crisis

## Late stage (severe)

**What it can look like:**

- Needing help around the clock with daily care
- Losing awareness of recent experiences and surroundings
- Trouble walking, sitting, and eventually swallowing
- Speaking very little, or not at all
- Being more likely to get infections, especially pneumonia

**What helps:**

- Comfort comes first: gentle touch, soft music, familiar voices, and keeping the person clean and comfortable
- Watching for pain, which the person may not be able to describe
- Help with eating and drinking, and asking about swallowing problems
- **Hospice care.** Hospice focuses on comfort and supports the whole family. Medicare covers hospice when a doctor believes a person likely has 6 months or less to live. Many families say they wish they had asked about it sooner.

## A note for families

Each stage brings its own losses, and it's normal to grieve along the way, even while your loved one is still here. Connecting with others who understand helps. The Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline, **1-800-272-3900**, is free and can help at every stage.
