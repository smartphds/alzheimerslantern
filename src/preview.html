What is Alzheimer's disease?

A plain-English explanation of what Alzheimer's is, what it does to the brain, how it changes over time, and what can help.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 18, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This article is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. Read our full disclaimer.

The short answer

Alzheimer's disease is a brain disease that slowly damages memory, thinking, and, over time, the ability to do everyday things. It is the most common cause of dementia. It causes an estimated 60 to 80 percent of all dementia cases.

It is not a normal part of getting older. Many people live into their 90s with sharp minds. When memory problems keep getting worse and start to get in the way of daily life, something more than aging is going on.

How common is it?

  • About 7.2 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's. That's about 1 in 9 people in that age group.
  • Almost two out of three Americans with Alzheimer's are women.
  • Nearly 12 million family members and friends give unpaid care to someone with Alzheimer's or another dementia.

If you're caring for someone, you are far from alone.

What happens in the brain

In 1906, a German doctor named Alois Alzheimer studied the brain of a woman who had died after years of memory loss and confusion. He found two kinds of damage that are still the hallmarks of the disease today:

  • Plaques. Clumps of a protein called beta-amyloid build up between brain cells.
  • Tangles. Twisted fibers of another protein, called tau, build up inside brain cells.

Together, these get in the way of how brain cells talk to each other. Over time, the cells lose their connections and die, and the brain shrinks.

The damage usually starts in the hippocampus, the part of the brain that forms new memories. That's why forgetting recent things is often the first sign. Later it spreads to areas that handle language, reasoning, and behavior, and eventually to areas that control basic body functions.

These brain changes can start 10 to 20 years before anyone notices a problem.

Early signs to watch for

Everyone forgets things sometimes. Signs that may point to something more include:

  • Forgetting recent conversations or events, and asking the same question again and again
  • Trouble planning, following a recipe, or keeping track of bills
  • Getting lost in familiar places
  • Trouble finding the right words
  • Misplacing things in odd places, like keys in the freezer
  • Poor judgment, such as falling for scams
  • Pulling away from hobbies or friends
  • Changes in mood or personality, such as becoming confused, suspicious, or anxious

If you notice several of these, talk to a doctor. Some memory problems are caused by treatable conditions, and an early diagnosis gives more time to plan.

The stages

Alzheimer's gets worse over time, but everyone moves through it differently. On average, people live 4 to 8 years after diagnosis, and some live 20 years or more.

  • Early stage (mild). The person can still drive, work, and socialize. They may struggle with names, recent events, or planning. This is the best time to make legal and money plans together.
  • Middle stage (moderate). Usually the longest stage. The person needs more help with daily tasks, may get confused about where they are, may wander, and may have mood and behavior changes.
  • Late stage (severe). The person needs help around the clock with eating, moving, and personal care, and may lose the ability to have a conversation.

Who gets it? Risk factors

There usually isn't one single cause. Things that raise the risk include:

  • Age. This is the biggest risk factor. Most people are diagnosed after 65. Rarely, it begins in a person's 30s, 40s, or 50s. This is called early-onset Alzheimer's.
  • Family history and genes. Having a parent or sibling with Alzheimer's raises your risk. A gene called APOE-e4 raises risk, but it doesn't mean a person will definitely get the disease.
  • Heart and body health. High blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, inactivity, untreated hearing loss, and social isolation are all linked to higher dementia risk.

Good news: a major 2024 report in The Lancet estimated that nearly half of dementia cases worldwide might be prevented or delayed by managing 14 risk factors. These include blood pressure, hearing, physical activity, and staying socially connected.

How it's diagnosed

There's no single simple test. A doctor usually:

  • Asks about symptoms and medical history, and talks with family
  • Gives memory and thinking tests
  • Orders blood tests to rule out other causes, such as thyroid problems or low vitamin B12
  • May order a brain scan (MRI, CT, or PET), a spinal fluid test, or one of the newer blood tests that look for Alzheimer's proteins

Ask for a referral to a neurologist, geriatrician, or memory clinic if you want a more detailed evaluation.

Treatment

There is no cure yet, but there are treatments that can help.

  • Medicines that ease symptoms. Donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, and memantine can help memory and thinking for a while in some people.
  • Newer medicines that slow the disease. Lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla) remove amyloid from the brain. They can modestly slow decline in people at the early stage. They are given by IV infusion, need regular brain scans, and can cause side effects such as brain swelling or small bleeds. They are not right for everyone.
  • Care that makes every day better. A steady routine, a safe home, good sleep, exercise, music, and staying connected with people all make a real difference, at every stage.

Ask the doctor which options fit your situation, and what the benefits and risks are.

What to do next

  • Worried about someone? Write down the changes you've noticed and when they started, and bring the list to a doctor.
  • Just diagnosed? Call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900. It's free, and you can call any time.
  • Want to understand the bigger picture? Read What is dementia?
  • Get our calm, practical letter every Friday: join the Friday letter.

What is dementia?

Dementia isn't one disease. Here's what the word really means, how it differs from Alzheimer's, and the main types, explained simply.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 18, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This article is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. Read our full disclaimer.

The short answer

Dementia is a general word for a loss of memory, thinking, and reasoning that is serious enough to get in the way of daily life. It can also change mood, personality, and behavior.

Dementia is not a disease by itself. It's an umbrella term, a bit like "heart disease." Many different conditions can cause it. Alzheimer's disease is the most common one.

Dementia vs. Alzheimer's: what's the difference?

Think of it this way:

  • Dementia describes the symptoms: memory loss, confusion, trouble with everyday tasks.
  • Alzheimer's is one specific disease that causes those symptoms. It causes 60 to 80 percent of dementia cases.

So everyone with Alzheimer's has dementia, but not everyone with dementia has Alzheimer's. Knowing the type matters, because the symptoms, the treatments, and even which medicines are safe can be different.

Is it just part of getting old?

No. Dementia becomes more common with age, but it is not a normal part of aging. Many people reach their 90s and beyond without it.

  • Normal aging: sometimes forgetting a name or appointment and remembering it later. Occasionally needing help with a new gadget.
  • Possible dementia: forgetting recent events often, repeating questions, getting lost in a familiar place, or not being able to manage bills or medicines that used to be easy.

The main types of dementia

  • Alzheimer's disease. The most common type. Protein build-up (amyloid plaques and tau tangles) slowly damages brain cells. It usually starts with trouble remembering recent things. Read our full guide to Alzheimer's.
  • Vascular dementia. Caused by reduced blood flow to the brain, often after strokes or from damaged blood vessels. Problems with planning, focus, and slowed thinking may show up more than memory loss. Symptoms can get worse in sudden steps.
  • Lewy body dementia. Caused by protein clumps called Lewy bodies. Common signs are seeing things that aren't there, good days and bad days, acting out dreams in sleep, and stiffness or shuffling like Parkinson's. People with this type can react badly to some medicines, so it's important to have the right diagnosis.
  • Frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Damages the front and side parts of the brain. It often starts younger, between 45 and 64. Early signs are usually changes in personality, behavior, or language rather than memory.
  • Mixed dementia. More than one type at once, most often Alzheimer's and vascular dementia together. This is common in people over 80.

Some causes can be treated

Not every memory problem is dementia. Some conditions cause similar symptoms and can get better with treatment:

  • Side effects of medicines, or mixing medicines
  • Thyroid problems
  • Low vitamin B12
  • Depression
  • Infections. In older adults, a urinary tract infection can cause sudden confusion.
  • Sleep problems such as sleep apnea
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus (extra fluid in the brain)

Sudden confusion that starts over hours or days is called delirium. It needs a doctor the same day. It's often caused by an infection, dehydration, or a medicine, and it can usually be treated.

How many people have dementia?

The World Health Organization estimates that more than 55 million people worldwide live with dementia. In the U.S., about 7.2 million people 65 and older live with Alzheimer's alone.

What to do if you're worried

  • Write down what you've noticed. Note what changed, when it started, and examples. This helps the doctor a lot.
  • Make a doctor's appointment and bring a list of all medicines, vitamins, and supplements.
  • Ask about tests that rule out treatable causes, and ask for a referral to a memory specialist if needed.
  • Get support early. The Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline, 1-800-272-3900, helps with all types of dementia, not just Alzheimer's.

10 early signs of Alzheimer's

Everyone forgets things. Here are the 10 warning signs that mean it's time to talk to a doctor, and how they differ from normal aging.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Normal forgetting vs. a warning sign

Forgetting where you put your glasses is normal. Forgetting what glasses are for is not. The difference is whether memory and thinking problems keep getting worse and get in the way of everyday life.

Below are the 10 warning signs the Alzheimer's Association lists. For each one, there's an example of what's typical with age, so you can compare. You can print this page and tick off what you've noticed before a doctor's visit.

1. Memory loss that disrupts daily life

Forgetting things you just learned, important dates, or events. Asking the same question again and again. Relying more and more on notes or family members for things the person used to handle.

Typical with age: sometimes forgetting a name or appointment, but remembering it later.

2. Trouble planning or solving problems

Difficulty following a familiar recipe, keeping track of monthly bills, or working with numbers. Things take much longer than they used to.

Typical with age: making an occasional mistake when balancing a checkbook.

3. Difficulty with familiar tasks

Getting lost driving to a familiar place, forgetting the rules of a favorite game, or struggling to use the microwave.

Typical with age: occasionally needing help with a new TV remote or phone setting.

4. Confusion about time or place

Losing track of dates, seasons, or how much time has passed. Forgetting where they are or how they got there.

Typical with age: getting confused about the day of the week, then figuring it out.

5. Trouble with vision and space

Problems judging distance, telling colors apart, or reading. This can lead to falls or trouble driving. (Always rule out eye problems, such as cataracts.)

Typical with age: vision changes from cataracts.

6. New problems with words

Stopping in the middle of a conversation with no idea how to go on. Struggling to find the right word or calling things by the wrong name, such as calling a watch a "hand clock."

Typical with age: sometimes having trouble finding the right word.

7. Misplacing things and not being able to retrace steps

Putting things in unusual places, such as keys in the fridge. Being unable to go back over their steps to find them. Sometimes accusing others of stealing.

Typical with age: misplacing things from time to time and retracing steps to find them.

8. Poor judgment

Giving large amounts of money to telemarketers or scams. Paying less attention to grooming or keeping clean.

Typical with age: making a bad decision once in a while.

9. Pulling away from work and social life

Losing interest in hobbies, social plans, or sports. Avoiding being social because of the changes they've noticed in themselves.

Typical with age: sometimes feeling tired of work or social obligations.

10. Changes in mood and personality

Becoming confused, suspicious, depressed, fearful, or anxious. Getting upset easily at home, at work, or anywhere outside their comfort zone.

Typical with age: having set ways of doing things and getting irritable when a routine is disrupted.

What to do if you notice these signs

  • Don't wait. Many things can cause memory problems, including medicine side effects, thyroid problems, low vitamin B12, depression, and infections. Some of these can be treated.
  • Write it down. List which signs you've noticed, when they started, and real examples. Bring the list to the appointment.
  • Make an appointment with a primary care doctor, and ask for a memory or cognitive evaluation. Medicare covers a yearly wellness visit that includes a check of memory and thinking.
  • Go together. A family member can share what they've seen and help remember what the doctor says.

If it is Alzheimer's, finding out early gives more time to plan, to try treatments that work best in the early stage, and to make decisions together. Read What is Alzheimer's disease? to learn more, or Your first 30 days after a diagnosis if you've just had the news.

The stages, explained simply

Alzheimer's usually moves through three broad stages. Knowing what each looks like helps you plan ahead and know what kind of help to look for.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Everyone's path is different

Alzheimer's gets worse over time, but at a different pace for each person. On average, people live 4 to 8 years after a diagnosis, and some live 20 years or more. Stages overlap, and a person may show signs of two stages at once. Use these as a rough map, not a timetable.

Doctors sometimes use a seven-stage scale. Most families find the simpler three stages below easier to follow.

Before symptoms: the hidden stage

Changes in the brain can begin 10 to 20 years before anyone notices a problem. Researchers call this "preclinical" Alzheimer's. Some people then have mild cognitive impairment (MCI): small memory or thinking problems that others may notice but that don't stop daily life. Not everyone with MCI goes on to develop Alzheimer's.

Early stage (mild)

What it can look like:

  • Trouble remembering names or recent conversations
  • Losing or misplacing things more often
  • Trouble planning, organizing, or managing money
  • Taking longer to finish everyday tasks
  • Still driving, working, and socializing, often well

What helps:

  • Make legal, money, and care plans now, while the person can take part and say what they want. See Power of attorney and advance directives.
  • Ask the doctor about treatments. The newer drugs that slow the disease are only for the early stage.
  • Keep active, social, and engaged. Exercise, hobbies, and friends all help.
  • Use memory aids: a large calendar, pill organizers, phone reminders.
  • Join a support group, for the person with the diagnosis and for family.

Middle stage (moderate)

This is usually the longest stage and can last many years. The person needs more and more help.

What it can look like:

  • Forgetting events or parts of their own history
  • Confusion about where they are or what day it is
  • Needing help choosing clothes, bathing, or using the toilet
  • Changes in sleep, such as sleeping during the day and being restless at night
  • Wandering and getting lost
  • Mood and behavior changes: suspicion, repeating questions, frustration, or agitation, often worse in the evening (sundowning)

What helps:

  • A steady daily routine
  • Simple, one-step instructions and calm communication
  • Making the home safe and planning for wandering
  • Getting help for yourself: adult day programs, in-home help, and respite care
  • Talking about future care options before a crisis

Late stage (severe)

What it can look like:

  • Needing help around the clock with daily care
  • Losing awareness of recent experiences and surroundings
  • Trouble walking, sitting, and eventually swallowing
  • Speaking very little, or not at all
  • Being more likely to get infections, especially pneumonia

What helps:

  • Comfort comes first: gentle touch, soft music, familiar voices, and keeping the person clean and comfortable
  • Watching for pain, which the person may not be able to describe
  • Help with eating and drinking, and asking about swallowing problems
  • Hospice care. Hospice focuses on comfort and supports the whole family. Medicare covers hospice when a doctor believes a person likely has 6 months or less to live. Many families say they wish they had asked about it sooner.

A note for families

Each stage brings its own losses, and it's normal to grieve along the way, even while your loved one is still here. Connecting with others who understand helps. The Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline, 1-800-272-3900, is free and can help at every stage.

Your first 30 days after a diagnosis

A diagnosis of Alzheimer's can feel overwhelming. This week-by-week list breaks the first month into small, doable steps. You don't have to do everything at once.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

First, breathe

Hearing "Alzheimer's" is a shock, for the person diagnosed and for the family. Feeling numb, scared, angry, or even relieved to finally have an answer are all normal. Nothing on this list has to happen today. Print it, and work through it a little at a time.

Week 1: Understand and take in the news

  • Write down your questions as they come up. There will be many. See Questions to ask the doctor.
  • Ask for a copy of the diagnosis and any test results for your records.
  • Learn the basics from trusted sources. Start with What is Alzheimer's disease? and The stages, explained simply.
  • Call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900. It's free. A specialist can answer questions and point you to local help. You can call just to talk.
  • Let yourself feel it. Take time together before making big decisions.

Week 2: Talk about care and treatment

  • Make a follow-up appointment to talk about treatment options. Ask whether the newer medicines that slow the disease might be right, and what the risks are. See New Alzheimer's treatments in plain English.
  • Review all medicines, including over-the-counter ones and supplements. Some can make confusion worse.
  • Ask about a care plan. Medicare covers a special visit for people with memory problems to make a care plan. Ask the doctor about a "cognitive assessment and care plan" visit.
  • Ask for referrals to a specialist, such as a neurologist, geriatrician, or memory clinic, if you haven't seen one.

Week 3: Legal and money planning

This matters most early, while the person can still understand and sign documents.

  • Find important papers: wills, insurance policies, bank and retirement accounts, deeds, and passwords. Keep them in one safe place.
  • Set up a power of attorney for money and for health care, and an advance directive (a living will). See Power of attorney and advance directives.
  • Consider meeting an elder law attorney, who can explain options for paying for care later. The Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) can help you find free or low-cost legal help.
  • Watch for scams. Set up alerts on bank accounts and consider adding a trusted person to view the accounts.

Week 4: Build your support team

  • Decide who to tell and how. See Telling family and friends.
  • Share the load. Make a list of tasks (rides, meals, bills, visits) and let people choose what they can do.
  • Find a support group for the person with the diagnosis, for caregivers, or both. See Support groups near you and online.
  • Talk about safety: driving, cooking, medicines, and what to do if the person gets lost. See When to stop driving and Wandering: prevention and what to do.
  • Plan something enjoyable. A diagnosis doesn't end a life. Many people live well for years after an early diagnosis.

For the person with the diagnosis

If you're the one who has been diagnosed: you still get to make choices about your life and your care. This is the best time to say what matters to you, so your family can honor it later. Many people find it helps to keep a routine, stay active, and talk with others who are living with Alzheimer's too.

Wandering: prevention and what to do

Most people with dementia will wander or get lost at some point, even in the early stage. Here's how to lower the risk today, and exactly what to do if it happens.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

If someone is missing right now

  • Search the immediate area quickly, for no more than 15 minutes. Check nearby places they like and the direction of their dominant hand, which people often follow.
  • Then call 911. Say the person has dementia and is a "vulnerable adult." You do not need to wait 24 hours.
  • Have ready: a recent photo, what they're wearing, their medical needs, and places they might try to go, such as a former home or workplace.
  • Have someone stay home in case they come back.
  • Most people who wander are found close to where they went missing, often within a mile and a half.

Why people wander

According to the Alzheimer's Association, 6 in 10 people with dementia will wander at least once. Common reasons:

  • Feeling lost or confused, even at home
  • Trying to "go home" or "go to work," following an old routine
  • Looking for a person or place from the past
  • Needs like hunger, thirst, or using the bathroom
  • Restlessness, boredom, or too much noise
  • Evening confusion (sundowning)

Warning signs to watch for

  • Coming back later than usual from a walk or drive
  • Talking about going to a past job or home
  • Pacing or restless movement
  • Trouble finding familiar places, such as the bathroom
  • Seeming lost in a new or crowded place

Steps to take today

  • ID they always wear. A medical ID bracelet with "memory loss" and your phone number. Programs like MedicAlert offer 24/7 help if someone is found.
  • Tell neighbors and local shops. Give them your number and ask them to call if they see your loved one out alone.
  • Keep a recent photo and a list of places they might go.
  • Use GPS if it fits. Phone location sharing, GPS watches, or shoe trackers can help find someone quickly. Some areas also have programs such as Project Lifesaver through local police.
  • Make doors less inviting. Simple locks placed high or low, door chimes, or alarms. A dark mat or a sign that says "STOP" in front of a door can also help. Never lock a person with dementia in alone. It's a fire risk.
  • Hide car keys if the person should no longer drive.

Lower the urge to wander

  • Keep a daily routine with regular exercise. Being tired in a good way helps.
  • Take care of basic needs: food, drink, bathroom, and comfort.
  • Avoid busy, noisy places that can cause confusion.
  • If they want to "go home," reassure them rather than correcting them: "You're safe here. Let's have some tea first."
  • Plan calming activities for the times of day they're most restless.

Sundowning: why evenings are hard

Many people with Alzheimer's become more confused, restless, or upset in the late afternoon and evening. This is called sundowning. Here's what may cause it and what helps.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

What sundowning looks like

As the day goes on, the person may:

  • Become more confused or anxious
  • Pace, wander, or try to leave ("I need to go home")
  • Get angry, suspicious, or tearful
  • Have trouble settling down or falling asleep
  • See or hear things that aren't there

It's common in the middle and late stages. It's not your fault, and it's not the person being difficult on purpose.

What may cause it

No one knows the exact cause, but these often play a part:

  • Tiredness at the end of the day
  • Changes in the body clock caused by Alzheimer's
  • Fading light and more shadows, which can be confusing or scary
  • A busy household in the evening, with dinner, TV, and people coming home
  • Unmet needs such as hunger, thirst, pain, needing the bathroom, or boredom
  • Infections, especially urinary tract infections, which can suddenly make confusion much worse

What helps

During the day:

  • Keep a steady routine for waking, meals, and bedtime.
  • Get outside or near a bright window in the morning. Daylight helps set the body clock.
  • Plan active things, like walks and chores, earlier in the day.
  • Limit long naps, especially late in the day.
  • Cut down on caffeine and sugar after lunch, and avoid alcohol.

In the late afternoon and evening:

  • Turn on lights before it gets dark to reduce shadows. Close curtains at dusk.
  • Keep things calm: soft music, less TV, fewer visitors.
  • Offer a simple, soothing activity, like folding towels, looking at photos, or a hand massage.
  • Serve a lighter dinner, earlier.
  • Check for pain, hunger, thirst, and the need for the bathroom.

When it happens:

  • Stay calm and speak softly. Your mood is contagious.
  • Don't argue or try to reason. Reassure instead: "You're safe. I'm right here."
  • Gently redirect to a favorite snack, song, or task.
  • Give space, if it's safe, and try again a few minutes later.
  • Keep a simple diary of when it happens and what came before. Patterns can show you what to change.

When to call the doctor

  • If the confusion is sudden or much worse than usual. This may be an infection or a medicine problem, which needs checking soon.
  • If sleep problems are wearing everyone out. Ask about safe options. Many sleep medicines can make confusion and falls worse in older adults, so ask before trying anything, including over-the-counter sleep aids.

Communication that works

As Alzheimer's changes how a person understands and uses words, the way we talk has to change too. These simple techniques reduce frustration on both sides.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why talking gets harder

Alzheimer's affects the parts of the brain that handle language. A person may struggle to find words, lose their train of thought, or not follow long sentences. They often still understand tone of voice, facial expression, and touch long after words get hard. That's where you can make the biggest difference.

Before you speak

  • Get their attention first. Approach from the front, say their name, and make eye contact.
  • Cut the noise. Turn off the TV or move somewhere quiet.
  • Get on their level. Sit down rather than standing over them.
  • Check your mood. Take a breath. A calm, warm face says a lot.

How to say it

  • Use short, simple sentences. One idea at a time.
  • Ask one question at a time, and give time to answer. Count to ten in your head.
  • Offer simple choices, not open questions. "Would you like the blue shirt or the red one?" works better than "What do you want to wear?"
  • Ask yes-or-no questions when choices become hard.
  • Break tasks into steps. "Put your arm in the sleeve." Then, "Now the other arm."
  • Use names, not "he" or "she." Say "your daughter Anna" rather than "she."
  • Show, as well as tell. Point, demonstrate, or hand them the object.
  • Speak slowly and clearly, but not like you're talking to a child.

What to avoid

  • Don't argue or correct. If they say it's 1975, arguing won't change their mind and will only upset them both. Go along with their reality when it does no harm.
  • Don't quiz. "Do you remember who I am?" can feel like a test they're failing. Say, "Hi Mom, it's your son, David."
  • Don't talk about them as if they're not there.
  • Don't say "Remember when...?" Instead, share the memory: "I was thinking about our trip to the lake."

When words don't work

  • Listen for the feeling behind the words. "I want to go home" may mean "I feel unsafe" or "I'm tired."
  • Respond to the emotion: "You sound worried. I'm here with you."
  • Use touch, if they like it: holding a hand, a hand on the shoulder.
  • Redirect gently to something pleasant: a snack, a walk, a favorite song.
  • Take a break and try again later if things are tense.

Be kind to yourself

You will lose patience sometimes. Everyone does. When it happens, step away for a minute, breathe, and come back. Repeating the same answer for the tenth time is love in action, even when it doesn't feel like it.

You are not alone

Nearly 12 million Americans care for a family member or friend with Alzheimer's or another dementia, without pay. Many say the stress is high, and caregivers often put their own health last. Taking care of yourself isn't selfish. It's how you keep going.

  • Feeling tired all the time, even after sleeping
  • Getting sick more often
  • Feeling hopeless, numb, or like nothing you do is enough

Paying for care: Medicare and Medicaid basics

Paying for Alzheimer's care is one of families' biggest worries. Here's what Medicare and Medicaid usually cover, the big gap many people don't expect, and where to get free help.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

The big surprise: long-term care

Many families assume Medicare will pay for help at home or a nursing home over the long term. It usually doesn't. Medicare does not pay for "custodial care," meaning ongoing help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, and eating, when that's the main care a person needs. Planning early for this gap is one of the most important things you can do.

Rules change and differ by state. Use this as a starting point, and check your own situation with the free help listed below.

What Medicare usually covers

  • Doctor visits, tests, and hospital stays
  • Memory evaluations and care planning. Medicare covers a visit to assess memory and thinking and make a care plan. Ask the doctor about a "cognitive assessment and care plan" visit.
  • Prescription drugs through Part D or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage
  • Short-term skilled nursing after a hospital stay of at least 3 days, in a skilled nursing facility, for up to 100 days. You pay a daily amount after day 20.
  • Some home health care if the person is homebound and needs skilled care, such as nursing or therapy
  • Hospice care when a doctor believes the person likely has 6 months or less to live. This covers comfort care, medicines for symptoms, equipment, and support for the family.

Dementia care programs: Medicare has been testing a program called GUIDE that offers care coordination, caregiver training, and up to $2,500 a year for respite care for people with dementia, through participating providers. Ask the doctor or your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (see below) whether it's available near you.

What Medicaid can cover

Medicaid is for people with low income and limited savings. Unlike Medicare, it can pay for long-term care, including:

  • Nursing home care, for people who qualify
  • Home and community-based services in many states, such as in-home help and adult day programs, through "waiver" programs

Things to know:

  • Each state sets its own rules for income and savings limits.
  • Medicaid looks back at money and property given away in the 5 years before applying. Gifts in that time can delay coverage.
  • A spouse who stays at home is protected from losing everything. There are special rules to help them keep some income and savings.

Because the rules are complicated, many families meet with an elder law attorney before applying.

Other ways families pay

  • Personal savings and income
  • Long-term care insurance, if the person bought a policy. Check what it covers and how to file a claim.
  • Veterans' benefits. Veterans and surviving spouses may qualify for extra money for care through the VA's "Aid and Attendance" benefit.
  • Life insurance policies can sometimes be used for care.
  • Home equity, such as a reverse mortgage. Get independent advice first.

Where to get free, unbiased help

  • SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program). Free, one-on-one Medicare counseling. Find yours at shiphelp.org.
  • Eldercare Locator: 1-800-677-1116. To find your local Area Agency on Aging, which knows local programs.
  • Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline: 1-800-272-3900.
  • Your state Medicaid office, for how to apply.

MCI or dementia: what's the difference?

Mild cognitive impairment, or MCI, sits between normal aging and dementia. Here's what it means, how it differs from dementia, and what to do next.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

What is MCI?

Mild cognitive impairment means a person has more trouble with memory or thinking than is normal for their age, but they can still handle everyday life on their own. Others may notice the changes, but the person can still drive, manage money, and take care of themselves.

How it differs from dementia

  • Normal aging: occasional forgetfulness, like misplacing keys or blanking on a name, that doesn't get worse over time.
  • MCI: noticeable problems, such as often forgetting appointments or conversations, but daily life still works.
  • Dementia: problems serious enough that the person needs help with everyday tasks, such as bills, medicines, cooking, or getting around. See What is dementia?

Will MCI turn into Alzheimer's?

Not always. Some people with MCI go on to develop Alzheimer's or another dementia. Others stay the same for years, and some even get better, especially when the cause is treatable.

MCI can be caused by:

  • Early Alzheimer's disease
  • Small strokes or blood vessel problems
  • Medicine side effects
  • Depression, anxiety, or stress
  • Poor sleep or sleep apnea
  • Thyroid problems or low vitamin B12

That's why a full check-up matters.

What to do

  • See a doctor for a full evaluation, and ask what might be causing the changes.
  • Ask about follow-up. Many doctors recheck memory every 6 to 12 months to see if anything changes.
  • Ask about treatment. If MCI is caused by Alzheimer's, the newer medicines that slow the disease may be an option. See New Alzheimer's treatments in plain English.
  • Protect your brain health: stay active, treat high blood pressure and diabetes, get hearing checked, sleep well, don't smoke, and stay social.
  • Plan ahead anyway. Setting up a power of attorney and advance directive is wise for everyone.

New Alzheimer's treatments in plain English

Two newer medicines can slow Alzheimer's in its early stage. They aren't a cure and aren't right for everyone. Here's what they do, who they're for, and what to ask.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Two kinds of Alzheimer's medicines

1. Medicines that ease symptoms. These have been used for many years. They don't change the course of the disease, but they can help memory, thinking, or behavior for a while in some people.

  • Donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine are used in mild to moderate (and sometimes severe) stages.
  • Memantine is used in moderate to severe stages, sometimes together with donepezil.
  • Common side effects include upset stomach, loss of appetite, dizziness, and sleep changes.

2. Medicines that slow the disease. These are newer and work in a different way. They target amyloid, the sticky protein that builds up in the brains of people with Alzheimer's.

The newer medicines

  • Lecanemab (brand name Leqembi), approved by the FDA in 2023
  • Donanemab (brand name Kisunla), approved by the FDA in 2024

In large studies, both modestly slowed the decline of memory and thinking over about 18 months in people with early Alzheimer's, compared with people who got a placebo. They do not stop the disease or bring back lost memory.

Who they're for

  • People with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia caused by Alzheimer's
  • Amyloid must be confirmed with a PET scan or a spinal fluid test
  • They're not for people in the moderate or late stage

How they're given

  • Usually by IV infusion at a clinic, every 2 weeks (lecanemab) or every 4 weeks (donanemab)
  • Regular MRI brain scans are needed to check for side effects

Side effects to know about

The main risk is called ARIA: swelling or small bleeds in the brain. Most of the time it causes no symptoms and goes away, but it can be serious, and rarely life-threatening.

  • People who carry two copies of a gene called APOE-e4 have a higher risk. Doctors may suggest a genetic test first.
  • People who take blood thinners may face extra risk.
  • Call the doctor right away about new headache, confusion, dizziness, vision changes, or nausea.

Questions to ask the doctor

  • Is this treatment an option for us? Why or why not?
  • What tests do we need first?
  • How much benefit might we expect, and how will we know if it's working?
  • What are the risks for us, given our genes and other medicines?
  • How often are infusions and MRIs, and where?
  • What will Medicare or our insurance cover, and what will we pay?

Other ways to help

Whatever treatment you choose, everyday things still matter a lot: exercise, sleep, social time, treating hearing loss, controlling blood pressure, and staying engaged. Clinical trials are also testing many new treatments. Ask the doctor, or search ClinicalTrials.gov.

Bathing without a battle

Bathing is one of the hardest daily tasks for people with Alzheimer's and their caregivers. Here's why it can be upsetting and simple ways to make it calmer and safer.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why bathing can be upsetting

To a person with Alzheimer's, a bath or shower can feel cold, scary, and confusing. They may feel embarrassed being undressed, frightened by running water, or not understand why they need to wash. Refusing a bath is common and usually comes from fear, not stubbornness.

Get ready first

  • Pick their best time of day. If they always bathed in the morning, keep it that way.
  • Warm the room and the towels.
  • Have everything ready: towels, soap, clean clothes, all within reach.
  • Check the water temperature with your elbow or a thermometer. Older skin burns easily.
  • Make it safe: a non-slip mat, grab bars, a shower chair, and a handheld shower head.

During the bath

  • Explain one step at a time: "Let's wash your arms now."
  • Let them do what they can, even if it takes longer.
  • Protect their privacy. Keep a towel over their shoulders or lap.
  • Use a gentle spray, or a washcloth, rather than water in the face.
  • Stay calm and warm, and talk about something pleasant.
  • Never leave the person alone in the tub or shower.

If they refuse

  • Don't force it. Try again in 15 to 30 minutes, or at another time.
  • Change the words: "Let's freshen up" or "The doctor said a warm wash would help your back."
  • A sponge bath in a warm room is fine on many days. Most people don't need a full bath every day. Two or three times a week is often enough.
  • Play their favorite music.
  • Ask whether someone else, like a home health aide, might have better luck.

After the bath

  • Dry well, especially skin folds, to prevent sores.
  • Check the skin for redness, rashes, or sores.
  • Use lotion on dry skin.
  • Help them dress in clothes laid out in order.

What to say when they ask for someone who has died

When a person with Alzheimer's asks where their late husband or mother is, the truth can feel like hearing the news for the first time. Here are gentle ways to respond.

Why it happens

Alzheimer's affects recent memories first. A person may remember their spouse or parent as still alive, and look for them, sometimes every day. Each time you say "she died," they may grieve as if it just happened.

Listen for the feeling

The question often means something more:

  • "Where's my mother?" may mean "I feel scared" or "I want comfort."
  • "When is John coming home?" may mean "I miss him" or "I feel lonely."

Responding to the feeling usually helps more than the facts.

Gentle ways to respond

  • Ask about the person: "Tell me about your mother. What was she like?"
  • Share a memory: "John loved this song, didn't he?"
  • Comfort: "You miss him. I'm here with you."
  • Redirect kindly: "Let's look at some pictures of your family," or offer a snack or walk.
  • Use a soft, true answer when you can: "He's not here right now."

Should I tell the truth?

There's no single right answer. In the early stage, honesty is usually best, said with care. Later on, many families and experts find that repeating painful news causes more harm than good. Choosing a kind, comforting answer is not lying to hurt. It's meeting the person where they are.

If the person seems to sense the truth or asks directly, it's okay to answer gently: "Your mother died a long time ago. She loved you very much."

Take care of yourself

Hearing these questions can be painful, especially if you're grieving the same person. It's okay to step away for a moment, and to talk with someone who understands.

When to stop driving

A diagnosis doesn't always mean someone must stop driving right away, but Alzheimer's will eventually make driving unsafe. Here are the warning signs and how to have the conversation.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why driving gets risky

Driving takes quick thinking, judgment, and memory of routes and rules. Alzheimer's slowly affects all of these. People in the early stage may still drive safely for a while, but that changes over time, often before the person notices.

Warning signs

  • Getting lost on familiar routes
  • Missing stop signs or traffic lights
  • Drifting into other lanes, or hitting curbs
  • Confusing the gas and brake pedals
  • New dents or scrapes on the car
  • Taking much longer to make a short trip
  • Getting angry or confused while driving
  • Close calls, tickets, or other drivers honking more often

If you're not sure, ride along a few times and watch.

Getting an outside opinion

  • Ask the doctor to talk about driving. Many people accept it better from a doctor.
  • Ask about a driving evaluation by an occupational therapist or driver rehabilitation specialist.
  • Check your state's rules. Some states require doctors to report certain conditions.

How to have the conversation

  • Start early, ideally soon after the diagnosis, before there's a crisis.
  • Talk about it as safety, not failure: "I want you to stay safe, and others too."
  • Involve the person in planning when to stop and how they'll get around.
  • Expect strong feelings. Driving can mean independence and pride. Grief and anger are normal.
  • Have a plan ready for rides: family, friends, volunteer drivers, senior transportation, ride services, or public transit.

If they keep driving when it's unsafe

  • Ask the doctor to write a "do not drive" prescription.
  • Keep the car keys out of sight.
  • Disable the car or ask a mechanic for help.
  • Sell or move the car, if needed.

Power of attorney and advance directives

Legal planning lets a person with Alzheimer's choose who will make decisions for them later. It needs to happen early, while they can still understand and sign. Here's what each document does.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why early matters

To sign legal documents, a person must understand what they're signing. Alzheimer's slowly takes that ability away. If papers aren't in place, families may have to go to court for guardianship, which is slow, costly, and stressful. Planning early also lets the person say what they want.

This is general information, not legal advice. Laws vary by state. An elder law attorney can help.

The key documents

  • Durable power of attorney for finances. Names a trusted person (the "agent") to manage money, pay bills, and handle property if the person can't. "Durable" means it stays in effect after the person loses the ability to decide.
  • Health care power of attorney (also called a health care proxy). Names someone to make medical decisions when the person can't.
  • Living will. Writes down wishes about medical care at the end of life, such as feeding tubes, breathing machines, and resuscitation (CPR).
  • POLST or MOLST form. For people who are seriously ill, a doctor fills out this medical order so emergency workers and hospitals follow the person's wishes.
  • Will. Says who gets property after death and who handles the estate.
  • Living trust (optional). Can hold property and make it easier to manage and pass on.

Steps to take

1. Talk together about wishes, values, and who should be in charge. 2. Gather papers: bank accounts, insurance, property, retirement accounts, and existing documents. 3. Meet an attorney, ideally one who works with older adults. Ask about costs upfront. 4. Sign the documents and give copies to the agents, doctors, and hospital. 5. Keep originals safe but easy to find.

Home care, assisted living, or memory care?

As Alzheimer's progresses, many families need more help than they can give alone. Here's how the main types of care compare, so you can choose what fits.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 7-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Start with the person's needs

Ask yourself:

  • How much help does the person need with daily tasks like bathing, dressing, eating, and the toilet?
  • Is it safe for them to be alone? For how long?
  • Do they wander, or have behaviors that are hard to manage?
  • How are you, the caregiver, holding up?
  • What can the family afford, and what help is available?

The main options

In-home care

  • Aides come to the home for a few hours a day up to around the clock.
  • "Companion" or "homemaker" help covers company, meals, and housework. "Personal care" aides help with bathing and dressing.
  • Good for: people who want to stay home, and families who can fill gaps.
  • Usually paid privately. Medicaid waivers or long-term care insurance may help.

Adult day programs

  • Supervised activities, meals, and social time during the day, at a center.
  • Good for: giving caregivers time to work or rest, and keeping the person active.
  • Often lower cost than in-home care for the same hours.

Assisted living

  • An apartment or room with meals, help with daily tasks, and staff on site.
  • Good for: people who need some help but not constant nursing care.
  • Check whether the community accepts residents with dementia and how it handles wandering.

Memory care

  • A secure unit or community designed for people with dementia, with specially trained staff and structured activities.
  • Good for: people in the middle and later stages who wander or need close supervision.
  • Usually costs more than standard assisted living.

Nursing home (skilled nursing)

  • 24-hour nursing care and supervision.
  • Good for: people with complex medical needs or who need help with almost everything.
  • Medicaid can pay for nursing home care for people who qualify. See Paying for care.

  • Is staff kind and patient? Do they speak to residents by name?
  • How many staff are on duty, day and night?
  • What training in dementia care do staff have?
  • Are residents engaged in activities, or sitting alone?
  • Is it clean, calm, and safe? Are exits secure?
  • How do they handle behaviors, medicines, and medical emergencies?
  • Visit more than once, including at mealtime and in the evening.

Getting help choosing

  • Your local Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator: 1-800-677-1116)
  • A geriatric care manager (also called an aging life care professional), for a fee
  • State inspection reports for assisted living and nursing homes
  • Medicare's Care Compare tool for nursing homes

Mealtimes, eating, and swallowing

Many people with Alzheimer's eat less, forget to eat, or struggle with utensils as the disease changes. Small changes at the table can make meals calmer and help them get enough food and fluids.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why eating can get hard

  • They may forget they haven't eaten, or that they just did.
  • Too many choices, noise, or a busy plate can be confusing.
  • Taste and smell may change, so food seems bland.
  • Dentures that don't fit, sore gums, or medicines can make eating uncomfortable.
  • Depression can lower appetite.
  • In later stages, chewing and swallowing get harder.

Ways to make meals easier

  • Keep it calm. Turn off the TV. Eat at the same time and place each day.
  • Keep the plate simple. Use a plain plate that contrasts with the food and table. Serve one or two foods at a time.
  • Try finger foods. Sandwiches cut in quarters, chicken strips, steamed vegetables, and fruit pieces are easier than using a fork.
  • Give time. Don't rush. Show them how by eating with them.
  • Offer small meals and snacks through the day if big meals are too much.
  • Check the temperature of food and drinks, since they may not notice something is too hot.

Drinking enough

People with dementia often don't feel thirsty. Offer small cups of water, juice, or soup often. Foods like watermelon, oranges, and soups add fluid too. Dehydration can cause sudden extra confusion.

Warning signs of swallowing trouble

Talk to the doctor if you notice coughing or choking during meals, a wet or gurgly voice after eating, food held in the mouth, or weight loss. The doctor may suggest a swallowing test with a speech therapist, and softer foods or thickened drinks.

When to call the doctor

  • Losing weight without trying
  • Not drinking for a day or more
  • Sudden refusal to eat, which can mean mouth pain or another illness

Up at 2 a.m., pacing, or sleeping all day and awake all night? Sleep changes are common with Alzheimer's, and they wear out caregivers fast. Here's what often helps.

Why sleep changes

Alzheimer's affects the parts of the brain that control the body clock. People may sleep more in the day, wake often at night, or get confused about whether it's day or night. Pain, needing the bathroom, medicines, and too little daytime activity can make it worse.

Daytime habits that help at night

  • Get morning light. Open curtains or sit outside early in the day.
  • Stay active. A walk, chores, or an activity they enjoy. Avoid long naps late in the day.

At bedtime

  • Keep the evening quiet. Soft music, a warm drink without caffeine, or a familiar TV show.
  • Make sure they use the bathroom before bed.
  • Keep the bedroom comfortable, with a night light so they can find their way.
  • Use the bed only for sleep, so it signals rest.

When they wake at night

  • Stay calm and speak softly. Don't argue about the time.
  • Check for simple needs: bathroom, thirst, hunger, pain, too hot or cold.
  • Gently guide them back to bed. If they're upset, a short walk or a quiet activity in dim light can help.
  • Keep doors and stairs safe in case they get up alone. See home safety, room by room.

Some sleep aids, including over-the-counter ones, can make confusion worse or raise the risk of falls. Ask the doctor to check for pain, depression, sleep apnea, or medicines that may be keeping them awake.

If nights are broken, find help so you can rest. A family member, a paid aide for a few nights, or respite care can make a real difference.

Bladder and bowel accidents are common as Alzheimer's progresses, and they're hard for everyone. Many causes can be helped. A calm, matter-of-fact approach protects the person's dignity.

Anger, agitation, and aggression

Shouting, pushing away, or lashing out can be frightening, especially from someone who was always gentle. It's the disease, not the person. Usually something is causing it, and finding that cause is the first step.

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Look for the cause

Agitation is often the person's way of telling you something is wrong. Common causes:

  • Pain or illness, like a toothache, constipation, or an infection
  • Basic needs: hunger, thirst, needing the bathroom, being tired, too hot or cold
  • Too much going on: noise, crowds, a busy room, too many questions
  • Fear or confusion: a new place, a new caregiver, not recognizing someone
  • Feeling rushed or bossed during care like bathing or dressing
  • Medicines, or a new medicine

What to do in the moment

  • Stay calm. Speak slowly in a low, gentle voice.
  • Don't argue, correct, or try to reason. Say "I can see you're upset" instead.
  • Give them space. Step back and don't grab or hold them.
  • Remove the trigger if you can: turn off the TV, move to a quieter room.
  • Change the subject: a snack, a song, a walk, or a favorite photo.
  • If you need to, leave the room for a minute to calm down, as long as they're safe.

  • Plan baths and outings for their best time of day.
  • Simplify tasks and give one step at a time.
  • Make sure they get exercise and time outside.

When they ask the same question again and again

"What time is it? When are we leaving? Where's my mother?" Hearing the same question twenty times a day can test anyone's patience. The person truly doesn't remember asking. Often there's a feeling behind the question that you can answer.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 4-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why it happens

Alzheimer's damages short-term memory first. Each time they ask, it feels to them like the first time. Repeating can also be a sign of worry, boredom, or not knowing what will happen next.

Answer the feeling

Ask yourself what they might be feeling. "When are we going?" may really mean "I'm anxious." Try:

  • "We have plenty of time. I'll tell you when it's time to go."
  • "You're safe here with me."

A calm, warm answer helps more than the facts.

Practical tips

  • Answer briefly and kindly, as if it's the first time.
  • Don't say "I already told you." It upsets them and doesn't help them remember.
  • Write it down. A note or whiteboard that says "Lunch with Sarah at 12" lets them check for themselves.
  • Don't announce plans too early. Tell them about an appointment shortly before you leave, not the day before.
  • Redirect. Offer a snack, a walk, a task, or music.
  • Look for a pattern. Does it happen at the same time each day? Being bored or tired can make it worse.

Take care of yourself

It's normal to feel frustrated. Step away for a moment when you need to. Talking with other caregivers in a support group can help you laugh about it instead of crying.

What these words mean

  • Hallucinations: seeing, hearing, or feeling things that aren't there, like a stranger in the room.
  • Delusions: firmly believing something untrue, like "My spouse is an imposter."
  • Paranoia: suspicion, often accusing others of stealing or lying.

Accusations of stealing often happen because they put something away and forgot where.

Rule out other causes first

Tell the doctor about any new hallucinations. Infections, dehydration, medicine side effects, and poor eyesight or hearing can all cause them. Vivid hallucinations early on can also be a sign of Lewy body dementia, which needs careful medicine choices.

How to respond

  • Don't argue. You can't talk them out of it, and arguing makes them more upset.
  • Respond to the feeling. "That sounds scary. I'm here and you're safe."
  • Help look for missing things. Learn their usual hiding spots. Keep spare keys and glasses.
  • Check the room. Shadows, mirrors, TV, and patterns on walls can look like people. Better lighting or covering mirrors may help.
  • Distract gently with an activity, a walk, or music.
  • Don't take accusations personally. It's the disease talking.

When it's not a problem

If the hallucination isn't upsetting them, like a friendly cat they think they see, you may not need to do anything.

When to get help

Call the doctor if hallucinations are new, frightening, or leading to unsafe behavior. Call 911 if anyone is in danger.

When they refuse help, care, or the doctor

"I don't need help." "I'm not going to the doctor." "I don't need a bath." Refusing help is one of the most common struggles caregivers face. Many people with Alzheimer's don't realize they've changed, so your help can feel like an insult.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Why they refuse

  • They may not know they're ill. The disease can damage the part of the brain that lets someone see their own changes. This isn't stubbornness or denial.
  • They want to stay independent, like anyone would.
  • They feel embarrassed, scared, or rushed.
  • They don't understand what you're asking.

What often helps

  • Don't argue about the diagnosis. You don't need them to agree that they have Alzheimer's.
  • Frame help as something else. "The doctor wants to check your blood pressure." "This helper is here to help me with the house."
  • Give simple choices. "Do you want to shower now or after breakfast?" rather than "Do you want to shower?"
  • Pick your timing. Try again later, when they're rested and calm.
  • Use a trusted messenger. Sometimes a doctor, pastor, or old friend gets a yes when family doesn't.
  • Start small. A helper for one hour a week, growing slowly, is easier than a big change.
  • Focus on what matters most. Safety and health come first. Let small things go.

For doctor visits

Book the visit, then tell them about it shortly before you go. Call the office ahead and share your concerns privately. Bring our questions to ask the doctor.

When safety is at risk

If they refuse care and are in danger, such as not eating, leaving the stove on, or getting lost, talk to their doctor, or call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to find local help.

Going to the hospital or ER

A hospital visit can be hard for someone with Alzheimer's. New places, noise, and strangers can cause fear and sudden confusion. Planning ahead and staying close helps a lot.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Pack a bag now, before you need it

Keep a ready bag by the door with:

  • A list of medicines and doses, allergies, and doctors' names
  • Copies of insurance cards, power of attorney, and advance directives
  • A short "About me" page: what they like to be called, how they communicate, what calms them, and what upsets them
  • Glasses, hearing aids, dentures, and chargers
  • A change of clothes, a familiar blanket or photo, and snacks for you

At the ER or hospital

  • Tell staff right away that the person has dementia. Don't assume it's in the chart.
  • Stay with them as much as you can, or take turns with family.
  • Be the voice. They may say "I'm fine" when they're not, or not explain pain.
  • Ask about delirium. It's a sudden spell of worse confusion that is common in hospitals. Keeping glasses and hearing aids on, keeping lights on in the day, and having familiar people nearby can help prevent it.
  • Ask before new medicines if they can cause confusion.

Before going home

Ask who will follow up, what changed in their medicines, and whether they need rehab or home health. If they stayed at least three days as an admitted patient, Medicare may cover a short stay in a skilled nursing facility. Our guide to paying for care explains how that works.

Caring for someone in the late stage

In the late stage of Alzheimer's, people usually need help with everything, including eating, moving, and using the bathroom. They may speak very little. The focus of care turns to comfort, dignity, and connection.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

What the late stage often looks like

  • Needing help with all daily care
  • Speaking only a few words, or none
  • Trouble walking, then sitting up, then holding up their head
  • Trouble swallowing
  • Sleeping much of the day
  • Getting infections more easily, especially pneumonia

Comfort care

  • Skin: Change their position at least every 2 hours if they're in bed or a chair, to prevent pressure sores. Keep skin clean and dry. Use pillows to cushion bony spots.
  • Mouth: Clean teeth or gums daily. Keep lips moist.
  • Eating: Offer soft or pureed foods and thickened drinks if the doctor advises it. Make sure they're sitting upright while eating and for 30 minutes after.
  • Pain: They may not be able to tell you they hurt. Watch for frowning, moaning, restlessness, or pulling away when touched, and tell the doctor.

Connecting without words

People in the late stage can still feel comfort and love. Hold their hand. Brush their hair. Play music they loved. Read to them, pray with them, or just sit together. Talk to them as you always have.

Decisions you may face

The doctor may ask about feeding tubes, hospital trips, and antibiotics for infections. Research shows feeding tubes generally don't help people with advanced dementia live longer or more comfortably. Careful hand feeding is often recommended instead. These are hard choices. Lean on their advance directive and what they would have wanted, and ask about hospice.

Hospice and palliative care for dementia

Hospice is care that focuses on comfort, not cure, near the end of life. Many families say they wish they had called hospice sooner. Here's what it is and how it works for someone with dementia.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Palliative care and hospice: the difference

  • Palliative care eases symptoms and stress at any stage of a serious illness. The person can keep getting other treatment too.
  • Hospice care is for people who are likely in the last months of life. It focuses only on comfort and quality of life, not on treating the disease.

Who can get hospice

Under Medicare, a person can get hospice when a doctor says their life expectancy is 6 months or less if the illness runs its usual course. With dementia this can be hard to predict. Signs it may be time include not being able to walk, speak, or eat well, losing weight, and repeated infections or hospital stays. If the person lives longer than 6 months, hospice can continue as long as the doctor recertifies them.

What hospice provides

  • A team of nurses, aides, a social worker, a chaplain, and volunteers
  • Medicines and equipment for comfort, such as a hospital bed
  • Visits at home, in a nursing home, or in assisted living
  • Short respite stays to give family a break
  • Grief support for the family after the death

What it costs

Medicare Part A covers hospice care for the terminal illness. Medicaid and many private plans also cover it. You can stop hospice at any time and go back to regular treatment.

How to start

Ask the person's doctor for a hospice evaluation. You can also call a hospice agency directly and ask them to assess. You can compare hospice providers on Medicare's Care Compare website.

Grief, before and after the loss

Alzheimer's is sometimes called "the long goodbye." Many caregivers grieve for years: for the person's memories, their old relationship, and the future they planned. Grief often continues after the death. All of these feelings are normal.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Grieving while they're still here

You may grieve each loss as it comes: the day they forget your name, the day they move to memory care, the last real conversation. This is sometimes called anticipatory grief. You might feel sad, angry, guilty, numb, or even relieved, sometimes all in one day.

What helps:

  • Name the feeling. "I'm grieving" is true even though they're still alive.
  • Talk with people who understand, like a caregiver support group.
  • Make room for joy. Moments of connection still happen, and it's okay to enjoy them.

After the death

  • Relief is normal. Feeling relief that their suffering, or your caregiving, has ended doesn't mean you loved them less.
  • Your days may feel empty. Caregiving may have filled your life for years. It can take time to find a new routine.
  • Take care of your health. See your own doctor. Grief can affect sleep, appetite, and health.
  • Hospice grief support is offered to families for about a year after a hospice death, even if you didn't use all of hospice's services.

When to get more help

Talk to a doctor or counselor if, after several months, you can't do daily tasks, feel hopeless, or can't stop thinking about the loss. If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline any time.

Moving to memory care: deciding and settling in

Deciding to move someone to memory care or a nursing home is one of the hardest choices a family makes. It is not giving up. For many people, it brings better safety and care, and lets you go back to being a spouse or child instead of only a caregiver.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 6-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Signs it may be time

  • Their safety is at risk at home, even with help: wandering, falls, or leaving the stove on
  • Their care needs are more than you can handle, especially at night
  • Your own health is suffering
  • Behaviors are getting harder to manage at home
  • The doctor or other family members are worried

How to choose a place

  • Visit more than once, at different times of day, including a mealtime.
  • Ask about staff training in dementia care and how many staff work each shift.
  • Ask how they handle wandering, agitation, and medical problems.
  • Watch how staff speak to residents. Are they patient and kind?
  • Ask what's included in the price and what costs extra.
  • For nursing homes, look up ratings and inspection reports on Medicare's Care Compare.

Paying for it

Medicare does not pay for long-term memory care or assisted living. Most families pay privately, use long-term care insurance, or qualify for Medicaid. See our guides to care options and paying for care.

Moving day and the first weeks

  • Bring familiar things: photos, a favorite chair, a quilt.
  • Tell staff about their routines, likes, and what calms them.
  • Keep explanations simple. Some families say "This is where you'll stay while the doctor helps you."
  • Expect an adjustment period. It may take weeks to settle.
  • Visit often, but ask staff how visits are affecting them at first.

About guilt

Almost every caregiver feels guilty at this step. Making sure someone gets the care they need is an act of love.

Caregiving from far away

If you live an hour or more away, you may feel helpless or guilty. But long-distance caregivers can do a lot: handle bills and paperwork, research services, support the main caregiver, and plan visits well.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Ways to help from a distance

  • Take on paperwork. Bills, insurance, Medicare and Medicaid forms, and calls to services can all be done remotely.
  • Find local help. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to find the Area Agency on Aging near your loved one.
  • Support the main caregiver. Call regularly, listen, pay for respite care, or arrange meal delivery.
  • Build a local team. Get phone numbers for neighbors, friends, and faith community members who can check in.
  • Get permission. Ask your loved one to sign forms that let doctors talk with you, and keep copies of key documents.

Making visits count

  • Plan ahead. Book doctor visits during your trip so you can go along.
  • Look around the home for safety problems, spoiled food, unopened mail, or unpaid bills.
  • Notice changes in weight, hygiene, and mood.
  • Give the main caregiver real time off while you're there.
  • Leave time just to enjoy being together.

Consider a care manager

A geriatric care manager, sometimes called an aging life care professional, is a nurse or social worker who can assess needs, arrange services, and check in for you. They charge by the hour, and the cost is usually not covered by Medicare.

Sharing care with siblings and family

Often one person ends up doing most of the caregiving while others help less, or not at all. Resentment can build fast. A clear plan, made together, can protect both your loved one and your family relationships.

By the Alzheimer's Lantern team · Last reviewed November 19, 2026 · 5-minute read

We are not medical professionals. This guide is general information, not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor about your own situation. In an emergency, call 911. Read our full disclaimer.

Hold a family meeting

  • Include everyone who should be involved. Use a video call for those far away.
  • If possible, include the person with Alzheimer's, especially in the early stage.
  • Start with facts: what the doctor said and what care is needed now.
  • Make a list of jobs, then let each person choose what they can do.
  • Write down the plan and share it with everyone.
  • Set a date to check in again, since needs will change.

Jobs anyone can take on

  • Driving to appointments
  • Managing bills, insurance, and paperwork
  • Sitting with your loved one so the main caregiver gets a break
  • Paying for respite care, meal delivery, or cleaning
  • Researching services and care options
  • Weekly phone calls with the person and the caregiver

Handling disagreements

  • Focus on what your loved one needs and would want.
  • Ask for specific help: "Can you take Mom to her Tuesday appointments?" works better than "I need more help."
  • Invite family to spend a full day caregiving, so they understand.
  • A social worker, counselor, or faith leader can help run a hard meeting.
